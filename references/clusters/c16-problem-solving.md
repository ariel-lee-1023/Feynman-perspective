# Supplementary teaching — prerequisites, laws and physical intuition

uid: c16
uid: c14
uid: c15

Feynman's three optional review lectures of December 1961 and the early-1962
lecture on dynamical effects supply the main material. The regular lectures on
relations between sciences, energy, gravitation and quantum behavior provide
adjacent depth. This module does not reproduce the editors' exercises or treat
their explanatory footnotes as Feynman's statements.

Load for a student who can repeat a formula but cannot use it, for an uncertainty
about physical signs or approximations, or for discouragement tied to relative
academic standing. Use [physical law](c13-seeking-new-laws.md) for theory testing
and [mathematics education](c17-newmath.md) for the function of notation. The
personal reassurance here is specific to a selected learning environment.

## 1. Choose the learner's actual problem

The review opening distinguishes students who are completely confused from those
who can spend the time investigating something interesting on their own. More
lecturing is not automatically better for both. For the student who is lost,
return to the prerequisites: the meaning and use of differentiation, integration,
vectors and changing vectors. For the student who can proceed, a self-chosen
problem can produce more learning than another review of material already heard.

The condition is readiness to do useful independent work, not an idealized identity
as a “visual learner” or a “Feynman-type thinker.” The next small performance is
the evidence: can the student identify what changes, calculate a simple derivative,
draw the vectors or explain why a result has the stated sign? If not, the activity
needs a different starting point. It does not need a more impressive vocabulary.

The lecture does not promise to transform any student into a problem solver by
giving a general recipe. Feynman says that rules can teach differentiation, but
the ability to analyze a fresh physical situation is harder to communicate. His
response is a sequence of examples. The examples let the learner notice how
the law is attached to a real situation and then try that attachment again.

## 2. Separate relative rank from the work

In “Caltech from the bottom,” the audience consists of students selected from
among the strongest pupils in many schools. A low place in that selected group
is not the same observation as being unable to learn physics. Feynman's joke
about half the group remaining below average makes the changed comparison group
visible; its practical point concerns selection and ranking, not a universal
theorem about arbitrary skewed grade distributions.

He then distinguishes the rational explanation from the emotional blow. Being
told the arithmetic does not guarantee that the feeling disappears. The possible
paths include continuing to learn, finding a different environment, and avoiding
a lifelong demand to be first in the most selective group available. This is
reassurance with a boundary, not a promise of a brilliant career.

For a present learner, ask what the actual work shows. One can identify a missing
prerequisite or choose a tractable exercise without diagnosing the person's
character or future. Do not copy historical assumptions about who attends the
institution into advice to a contemporary audience.

## 3. Recover the meaning before moving symbols

In “Laws and Intuition,” a table of equations is not yet a working knowledge of
physics. Every letter refers to an object or a measurable quantity. Coordinates,
the system being described and the applicable approximation come first.

For an ideal spring, choose a displacement from equilibrium. If it is stretched
to the right, the restoring force points left. Work must be done to stretch it,
so its stored energy rises with the magnitude of displacement. These observations
recover the signs without trusting a remembered minus sign. The physical argument
is the memory aid. It is also a check on an equation copied from elsewhere.

A coordinate convention can reverse the sign of a component without reversing
the physical force. A constant can shift the zero of a potential without changing
the energy difference used in the calculation. The important question is which
relations change and which predictions remain the same. Do not treat arbitrary
notation as a disagreement about nature.

The review places more general relations beside their low-speed forms. State the
approximation before using the simpler expression. For a new problem, a useful
sequence is to identify quantities, choose the relation, state its conditions,
and inspect a simple limit. If the result is physically absurd, recheck the
model and algebra instead of adding a verbal excuse to the answer.

## 4. Account for the energy that the simple model leaves out

A sliding block can lose kinetic energy while the full system retains total
energy in other forms. The simplified mechanical account may exclude internal
motion, heat and deformation. That is a boundary of the account, not an exception
that destroys the conservation principle.

The review's discussion of friction uses a round trip. Dragging the object back
requires further work; the work does not vanish because the object returns to
its starting position. A position-only potential cannot supply that description.
In ordinary introductory terms, such a frictional force is nonconservative.

This example should prevent two opposite mistakes. Do not insist that every
mechanics calculation conserves the visible sum of kinetic and potential energy.
And do not infer that any decrease in that sum contradicts conservation of energy.
Specify the system, the forms retained, and what crosses the boundary.

## 5. Let different tools answer different questions

The examples in the review include particle motion, machine design, escape,
scattering and rockets. They illustrate choosing a relation that makes the
desired result accessible. A conservation argument can avoid a detailed time
history when only the relation between endpoints is wanted. A question about
motion through time can require a force law and integration. Numerical work is
another tool when a convenient closed expression is unavailable.

None of this licenses skipping necessary mathematics. Intuition suggests what
an answer should do; equations can keep track of relations too complicated to
hold entirely in a picture. Likewise, getting a number from an equation does
not show that the equation describes the system in question.

Use a new example to make this distinction visible: ask what must be known to
predict a stopping distance versus the exact stopping-time curve. Then determine
which supplied facts and assumptions support each calculation. Keep this a new
illustration, not an anecdote attributed to the historical lecture.

## 6. Put an analogy's failure to work

The regular energy lecture uses an account that remains constant as the visible
pieces are hidden or transformed. Its point is a quantitative relation, not a
literal substance made of bookkeeping counters. A helpful analogy carries a
specified relation and then stops.

The gravitation and quantum lectures similarly distinguish a successful relation
from a complete underlying mechanical explanation. In quantum discussion, bullets
and waves are comparisons used to reveal where expectations fail. Requiring every
phenomenon to behave like a familiar small machine would obstruct the lesson.

The relation-to-other-sciences lecture broadens the questions physics can touch,
but the historical assessments of those fields are dated. A host answering a
modern geology or biology question needs present evidence rather than a 1960s
statement about what remains unexplained.

## 7. How to keep the teaching voice useful

The review addresses the student directly and sometimes jokes about the difficulty
of teaching or the emotional effect of rank. Its warmth does not erase the need
for work. Its bluntness does not justify insulting someone who is confused.

Start with the specific difficulty. Work one example far enough that the student
can perform a check. Return the next move to them. If they are ready, change one
condition; if they are not, locate the prerequisite. Do not end with a claim that
understanding the explanation is equivalent to being able to solve new problems.

## 8. Boundaries

- A problem-solving example is not a universal pedagogy or a guarantee of transfer.
- Relative rank is not a clinical assessment or a career prediction.
- A coordinate sign is not a physical direction until the convention is specified.
- Conservation statements require a system and the relevant forms of energy.
- A successful mathematical relation need not have a familiar mechanical picture.
- Historical instrument descriptions and claims about then-current technology are
  not current product or engineering guidance.

For period and attribution conditions, return to [scope](../scope.md). Exact
equations and numerical exercises require a reliable technical source; this
module preserves reasoning operations rather than an OCR-derived formula sheet.
